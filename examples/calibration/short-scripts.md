# Six ways to tell one small story

Premise: a creator keeps changing a project's font instead of publishing. Every character and event below is fictional. These are original drafts, not claims about the user's life. Roughly 20–35 seconds each depending on delivery; no audio has been generated or timed.

Rate exact lines, not just whole scripts. Topic and premise stay similar; structures intentionally differ. Follow-up tests should isolate one dimension at a time.

## A — Scene first, dry

The video was ready.

Then the title needed a better font.

Then the new font needed a new color.

By midnight, the video had a beautiful title and no audience.

The next morning, the creator opened the file again.

One last change.

The title now said: “Final. Seriously.”

**Delivery:** Matter-of-fact. Leave a small beat before the final title. **Visual:** File versions accumulate while the preview stays paused.

## B — Blunt, direct address

Your project doesn't need another font.

Open the last three versions. What actually changed?

If the answer is “mostly the title,” close the font menu.

Watch the thing once.

Fix anything that makes it hard to follow.

Then publish the damn video.

You can learn more from one confused viewer than another evening of moving letters around.

**Delivery:** Direct, not shouted. **Visual:** Compare versions, then show the one meaningful clarity fix.

## C — Quiet, character observation

Every night, Sam opened the same project.

A little more spacing. A slightly warmer blue.

His sister asked when she could see it.

“Soon.”

She asked again a week later.

This time, he sent the unfinished version.

She watched it twice.

Then she called to ask how he'd made the opening.

He'd almost deleted that part.

**Delivery:** Warm and unhurried. **Visual:** Small real actions carry the feeling; avoid motivational stock footage.

## D — Small mystery

Two files. Same video.

One had the better font.

The other had six comments asking how it was made.

The difference?

That one had been published.

The polished version was still sitting on the desktop, waiting for a better opening.

And the creator had just found a new font.

**Delivery:** Curious, then dry. **Visual:** Reveal where each file lives. **Note:** The six comments are fictional, not performance evidence.

## E — Demonstration, no personal claim

Here's the same sentence in five fonts.

Pick the one that tells you what happened.

You can't. The sentence says, “An important event occurred.”

Now change the words:

“The client called while the export was failing.”

Keep the plain font.

There's something to watch now.

**Delivery:** Show-and-tell, conversational. **Visual:** Let the wording change be the main event.

## F — Comic escalation, stronger language

Alex was going to post on Monday.

Tuesday, he bought a font.

Wednesday, the font needed a brand system.

Thursday, the brand needed a launch strategy.

By Friday, he had a fucking mission statement.

Still no video.

On Saturday, his friend posted a shaky clip of a sandwich.

Alex started researching sandwich fonts.

**Delivery:** Deadpan; do not punch every line. **Visual:** The planning documents become more elaborate while the upload remains empty.

## Feedback to collect

- Favorite and least favorite version; exact lines you would keep or remove.
- Did the story feel observed, forced, preachy, or too neat? Where?
- Did the profanity add anything? Would another delivery change the answer?
- Which ending made you want to continue thinking, and which felt like a slogan?

Next round: keep the preferred form but change the subject. Compare clean and blunt takes of the same text separately from choosing a voice.
